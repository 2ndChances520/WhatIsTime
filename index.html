Part 2: The Mystery of Time

Everything in quantum physics changes with time. Particles decay, atoms stop vibrating, and waves collapse. Time is everywhere in the equations, serving as the clock that makes quantum mechanics work. Yet, unlike matter made of atoms or light made of photons, time is not made of anything.

In standard quantum mechanics, time is an external background, a stage where the drama of physics plays out. In Einstein's General Relativity, however, time is a dynamic, physical fabric woven into space (spacetime) that bends and stretches around massive objects.

This is the paradox: our most accurate atomic clocks can measure time to 18 decimal places, yet physics cannot fully explain what time is. It remains one of the greatest unresolved problems in physics.

While the physics of time points to mysteries at the Planck scale, Scripture shows us who stands beyond it. Below, we look at God beyond human time, the nature of eternity, and day and night in Heaven, and we close Part 2 with a verse comparing a day in God's presence to earthly time.

Part 3: Man Discovers the Precision of Time Through God's Creation of Cesium-133

What Time Do You Go By?

In my house, we have a running joke: "Are we going by microwave time or stove time?" No matter how many times I try to sync them, the clock on the microwave and the clock on the stove always end up a minute or two apart. It's a funny, daily reminder that even under the same roof, time isn't always agreed upon. It raises a fascinating question: what time do you actually go by?

While we might playfully argue over household appliances, physicists and philosophers have sorted time into a few frameworks to help us understand how it operates in the universe and in our minds:

  • Spacetime: Einstein's revelation that the three dimensions of space and the one dimension of time are woven together into a single, dynamic fabric that bends and warps around massive objects.
  • Proper Time: The time measured by a clock following a specific path through spacetime. Your proper time ticks at a different rate than someone else's, depending on gravity and how fast you are moving.
  • Manifest Time: The common-sense, sequential flow of time as we experience it day to day: the one-way march from the past, through the present, and into the future.
  • Psychological Time: How our brains perceive the passage of time. Five minutes in traffic can feel like an eternity, while a joyful weekend with loved ones vanishes in seconds.
  • Heaven Time: Revelation 22:5 says there will be "no more night" and that believers will reign "forever and ever," pointing to an existence that transcends finite earthly measurement.

But when science needs an absolute, universal standard, far more precise than our kitchen microwave, it looks to the building blocks of God's creation.

The Discoveries of Man

While God set the foundations of time and matter, humanity has spent centuries trying to comprehend them. We have studied the atom and the laws of the universe, but how did we discover the mathematical precision of time?

Socrates (c. 470–399 BCE) & Aristotle (384–322 BCE)

The philosophical inquiry into time began in ancient Greece. Socrates (often through the writings of his student Plato) viewed time as a moving image of eternity, a divine rhythm set by the perfect motion of the cosmos. For him, time was connected to the eternal realm of perfect forms. Aristotle took a more grounded, analytical approach. He defined time as the "measure of motion" in terms of a before and an after. If nothing changed, Aristotle argued, time would cease to exist. Their differing opinions set the stage: is time a divine framework, or just a byproduct of physical movement?

Dionysius Exiguus (525 AD)

In 525 AD, a monk named Dionysius Exiguus changed how we measure time. Tasked with calculating the date of Easter, he refused to use a calendar anchored to a Roman emperor who persecuted Christians. Instead, he reset the clock to the arrival of Jesus Christ, dividing all of earthly time into BC (Before Christ) and AD (Anno Domini, "in the year of our Lord").

In modern times, there has been a push to replace these abbreviations with BCE (Before Common Era) and CE (Common Era) to secularize the calendar. Yet despite the renaming, the reality remains: the entire world still numbers its days from the same origin point. We still use the timeline he began, simply calling it something different.

It is a reminder of Ecclesiastes 3. Our lives are bound by seasons, including "a time for war and a time for peace," yet time itself is anchored by the One who stepped into our timeline, inviting us out of temporary struggles and into His eternal peace.

Isaac Newton (1642–1727): Absolute Time

Centuries later, Sir Isaac Newton brought complex mathematics to the mystery. He believed in "Absolute Time," a master clock ticking uniformly across the universe, independent of the objects moving within it. To Newton, time was the rigid stage upon which God's creation played out.

Albert Einstein (1879–1955): Spacetime & Relativity

Einstein shattered Newton's rigid stage. Through his theories of Special and General Relativity, he proved mathematically that time is not absolute but relative. It bends, warps, and slows down near massive objects or at high speeds. Time and space are woven together into a single fabric called spacetime. A second for someone traveling near the speed of light ticks differently than a second for someone standing on Earth, a realization that echoes the scriptural truth that a thousand years to us is like a day to God.

The Quantum Era to Present

Today, modern physicists explore the quantum realm, dealing with Planck time (10-43 seconds) and the very fabric of reality. With tools like the Cesium-133 atom, we don't just philosophize about time. We measure it to 18 decimal places. Yet the more mathematically complex our formulas become, the more they reveal an intricate, breathtaking design authored by the Master Builder.

Part 5: The Science Parallel – Dimensions, Gravity & Slowing Time

The Dimensions in Physics

  • 1st Dimension: a single line, representing length.
  • 2nd Dimension: a flat plane, like a drawing on paper, adding width.
  • 3rd Dimension: our physical world, adding depth: up, down, left, right, forward, backward.
  • 4th Dimension: time. As Einstein showed, time is linked to our 3D space, creating the 4D fabric of spacetime that governs motion and decay.

The Fifth Dimension

Is there something beyond time and space? Mathematically, yes. In 1921, physicist Theodor Kaluza and mathematician Oskar Klein introduced the Kaluza-Klein theory, which unified gravity and electromagnetism by adding a fifth dimension to Einstein's equations. Modern String Theory and M-Theory require even more dimensions (10 or 11) for the mathematics of the universe to hold together.

The fifth dimension represents a realm outside our observable spacetime. In a 5D space, you could see our entire 4D timeline, past, present, and future, at once, as a single completed geometry. What physicists describe as a higher-dimensional reality, believers may recognize as eternity: the high and holy place where God resides, observing the Alpha and the Omega in a single, unified glance.

Can Time Stop or Slow Down?

We often think of gravity as a force pulling objects toward the ground. In Einstein's universe, gravity is more profound: gravity is the curvature of spacetime itself.

Imagine placing a heavy bowling ball on a trampoline. The ball creates a deep curve in the fabric, and a marble rolled nearby follows that curve. Massive objects like Earth, the Sun, and black holes do the same thing to the fabric of space and time.

Gravitational Time Dilation

Because gravity warps spacetime, it affects not only where objects move but also how fast time "ticks."

The rule: The stronger the gravity (the deeper the curve), the slower time passes.

If you stood on a massive planet with intense gravity, your clock would tick slower than the clock of an astronaut floating in deep space, far from any gravitational pull. Time slows down as you approach a massive source of gravity.

Fun Fact: Your Feet Are Younger Than Your Head!

Earth's gravity is slightly stronger at your feet than at your head, because your feet are closer to the center of the planet. That means time ticks a tiny bit slower at your feet, so they age slightly less than your head does.

The difference is minuscule. Over a lifetime, it adds up to only about half a microsecond. But it is real: in 2010, scientists at NIST used atomic clocks to measure time running faster when one clock was raised just 33 centimeters (about a foot) higher. Even your own body is a living demonstration that time bends with gravity.

It is a breathtaking reminder that even the "constants" we rely on are part of a dynamic, interconnected design where nothing is truly static, except for the One who holds the fabric together.

When I worked for Lovell Custom Sheet Metal, John Panda was the foreman of the composite shop, and I was his understudy. In the shop, we prefabricated the entire building, and precision was everything. As John used to say, "We work in 64ths of an inch in the composite shop, bud." If you were off by just 1/8 of an inch early on, by the time you finished the wall, the panels would be out by feet.

Later, I was out in the field working on the James River Assembly wall that faces the highway. The structural framing we were attaching to wasn't perfectly level, so the prefabricated panels weren't lining up. The field workers were getting impatient. They just wanted to get it done and suggested we drill new holes in the panels.

Remembering my training with John Panda, I knew that slipping by an eighth of an inch here and there would make the whole wall a disaster. So I made them stop. We took the panels down, went back to the framing, leveled it, and did it the right way. Once we laid the right groundwork, everything lined up perfectly.

But God is like the standard in the composite shop. He says, "No, we're going to do this right. We have to take it back to the foundation." He doesn't just want the final result; He wants us.
