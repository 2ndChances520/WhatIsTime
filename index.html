Part Two: The Mystery of Time

Everything in quantum physics changes with time. Particles decay, atoms vibrate, and waves collapse. Time is everywhere in the equations, serving as the clock that makes quantum mechanics work. Yet, unlike matter made of atoms, or light made of photons, time is not made of anything.

In standard quantum mechanics, time is an external background—a stage where the drama of physics plays out. But in Einstein's General Relativity, time is a dynamic, physical fabric woven into space (spacetime) that bends and stretches around massive objects.

This paradox—that our most accurate atomic clocks can measure time to 18 decimal places without modern physics being able to fully explain what time is—remains the greatest unresolved problem in physics.

While the physics of time points to mysteries at the Planck scale, the flow of time leaves behind a physical record of humanity. Below, we trace how time has preserved the most studied collection of ancient texts in human history: The Bible. By mapping each book through time, authorship, and the physical manuscript evidence that survived the centuries, we see a remarkable timeline of preservation.

What Time Do You Go By?

In my house, we have a running joke: "Are we going by microwave time or stove time?" No matter how many times I try to sync them up, the clock on the microwave and the clock on the stove always end up a minute or two apart. It’s a funny, daily reminder that even under the exact same roof, time isn't always agreed upon. It begs a fascinating question: What time do you actually go by?

While we might playfully argue over household appliances, physicists and philosophers have categorized time into a few distinct frameworks to help us understand how it truly operates in the universe and in our minds:

  • Spacetime: Einstein's revelation that the three physical dimensions of space and the one dimension of time are inextricably woven together into a single, dynamic fabric that bends and warps around massive objects.
  • Proper Time: The exact, physical time measured by a clock following a specific path through spacetime—meaning your "proper time" will literally tick at a different rate than someone else's depending on gravity and how fast you are moving.
  • Manifest Time: The common sense, sequential flow of time exactly as we experience it day-to-day—the seemingly unyielding, one-way march from the past, through the present, and into the future.
  • Psychological Time: How our brains perceive the passage of time. A five-minute wait in traffic can feel like an eternity, while a joyful weekend with loved ones vanishes in seconds.
  • Heaven Time: Revelation 22:5 notes there will be "no more night" and believers will reign "forever and ever," indicating an existence that transcends finite earthly measurements

The Discoveries of Man

While God set the foundations of time and matter, humanity has spent centuries trying to comprehend it. We have built up the creation of the atom and the laws of the universe, but how did we discover the mathematical precision of time?

In 525 AD, a monk named Dionysius Exiguus forever changed how we measure time. Tasked with calculating the date of Easter, he refused to use a calendar anchored to a Roman emperor who persecuted Christians. Instead, he reset the clock to the arrival of Jesus Christ, dividing all of earthly time into BC (Before Christ) and AD (Anno Domini, In the Year of Our Lord). In modern times, there has been a push to replace these acronyms with BCE (Before Common Era) and CE (Common Era) to secularize the calendar. Yet, despite renaming the era, the profound reality remains: the entire world still numbers its days from the exact same origin point. We still use the timeline he originated, simply calling it something different. It is a profound reminder of Ecclesiastes 3. While our lives are bound by inevitable seasons—"a time for war and a time for peace"—time itself is ultimately anchored by the One who stepped into our timeline, inviting us out of temporary struggles and into His eternal peace.

Socrates (c. 470–399 BCE) & Aristotle (384–322 BCE)
The philosophical inquiry into time began in ancient Greece. Socrates (often through the writings of his student Plato) viewed time as a moving image of eternity—a heavenly, divine rhythm dictated by the perfect motion of the cosmos. For him, time was deeply connected to the eternal realm of God's perfect forms. Aristotle, however, took a more grounded, analytical approach. He defined time simply as the "measure of motion" regarding a before and an after. If nothing changed, Aristotle argued, time would cease to exist. Their differing opinions set the stage: is time a divine framework, or just a byproduct of physical movement?

Isaac Newton (1642–1727) - Absolute Time
Centuries later, Sir Isaac Newton introduced complex mathematics to the mystery. He believed in "Absolute Time," describing it as a master clock ticking uniformly across the universe, completely independent of the objects moving within it. To Newton, time was the rigid stage upon which God's creation played out.

Albert Einstein (1879–1955) - Spacetime & Relativity
Einstein shattered Newton's rigid stage. Through his theories of Special and General Relativity, Einstein proved mathematically that time is not absolute, but relative. It bends, warps, and slows down near massive objects or at high speeds. Time and space are woven together into a single fabric called spacetime. A second for someone traveling near the speed of light ticks differently than a second for someone standing on Earth—a profound realization that perfectly echoes the scriptural truth that a thousand years to us is like a day to God.

The Quantum Era to Present
Today, modern physicists explore the quantum realm, dealing with Planck time ($10^{-43}$ seconds) and the very fabric of reality. With tools like the Cesium-133 atom, we don't just philosophize about time—we measure it to 18 decimal places. Yet, the more mathematically complex our formulas become, the more they reveal an intricate, breathtaking design authored by the Master Builder.

Part 5: Can Time stop or slow down?

When we think of gravity, we often think of a force pulling objects toward the ground. But in the context of Einstein's universe, gravity is much more profound: Gravity is the curvature of spacetime itself.

Imagine placing a heavy bowling ball on a trampoline. The ball creates a deep curve in the fabric. If you roll a marble nearby, it follows that curve. Massive objects like Earth, the Sun, and Black Holes do the exact same thing to the fabric of time and space.

Gravitational Time Dilation

Because gravity is the warping of spacetime, it doesn't just affect where objects move; it affects how fast time "ticks." This phenomenon is known as Gravitational Time Dilation.

The Rule: The stronger the gravity (the deeper the curve), the slower time passes.

This means that if you were standing on the surface of a massive planet with intense gravity, your clock would tick slower than the clock of an astronaut floating in deep space, far away from any gravitational pull. Time literally slows down relative to Earth as you approach a massive source of gravity!

It is a breathtaking reminder that even the "constants" we rely on are part of a dynamic, interconnected design where nothing is truly static—except for the One who holds the fabric together.
